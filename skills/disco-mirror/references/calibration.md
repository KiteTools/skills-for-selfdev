# Calibration and portrait

## Keep distinct working layers

Track the desired build, concrete episodes, hypotheses, confidence, and unresolved alternatives in the conversation. For each episode note situation, observable response, the user's exact inner phrase if given, bodily signal if given, and consequence. Do not invent an inner phrase and then cite it as the user's testimony.

Use this order of evidential weight: repeated specific episodes across contexts; a concrete free-text account; confirmation or rejection of a particular prediction; a short choice without explanation; desired self-image. A direct counterexample lowers support. Emotional resonance alone does not raise confidence.

- High confidence in this interview: two concrete episodes in different settings plus consistent feedback.
- Medium: one episode plus confirmation, or several consistent short answers.
- Low: aspiration alone, a single choice, or conflicting evidence.

These labels describe the conversation's support, not scientific accuracy.

## Adaptive questions

For “partly,” separate where the behavior occurs from where it reverses. For “no,” withdraw the prediction and test a nearby explanation. If a vivid sentence resonates, ask which part fits before expanding it. Avoid treating the same mechanism as confirmed merely because you renamed it.

A fictional calibration example: the model predicts rapid public disagreement; the user describes quietly postponing a reply. Withdraw the public-disagreement prediction. Ask whether the delay involves rehearsing arguments, gathering evidence, or moving attention elsewhere. Those alternatives should not be ranked as better or worse.

## Narrative portrait

Write a connected portrait proportional to the evidence, normally up to 1,000–1,800 words for a full interview and shorter when support is limited. Include:

1. A recognizable overall picture: where attention goes, what the person contributes, and the benefit and cost of the same mechanism.
2. The inner council: its organizing role, supporting voices, outward presentation, resource management, and late signals. A few voice names may help; do not expose the full ranking by default.
3. A working cycle: signal → interpretation → decision → expression → effect. Show the costly version of that cycle using supported situations.
4. Five to eight recognizable reactions across contexts when evidence permits. Include a correction made because of the user's counterexample if there was one; never fabricate a correction for the format.
5. Strengths tied to tasks and the conditions under which those strengths work well.
6. Recurring costs and early warning signs, with tentative wording where needed.
7. What is supported, inferred, or unresolved; where the desired build and reported behavior differ; a viable alternative explanation.

Use at most a few exact user phrases, only where they organize the result. Avoid universal praise and deterministic labels. Never fill a required count by inventing patterns.

## Optional build disclosure

Keep behavioral hypotheses flexible during the interview. Only at the end map them onto four attributes in the range 1–6 totaling 12. The signature is the recurring organizing voice, not automatically the highest number. Show alternatives when support is mixed. On request disclose attributes, signature, prominent and late voices, confidence, supporting episodes, and differences from the desired build. These are a metaphorical summary, not measurements.

## Optional balancing

Select one or two late signals whose absence repeatedly increases the cost of a strength. Do not simply target the lowest score. For each, name the late signal, the loop it may interrupt, a small observable experiment, and the possible cost of overcorrecting. Make the experiment answerable by experience rather than requiring agreement with the portrait.
